Phase 1 is done and committed as 1fa19ed, together with the package-vetting rule changes. No new packages were needed.

What’s built: a fluid-properties module with lookups by pressure and enthalpy, pressure and temperature, or pressure and entropy, plus saturated-liquid and saturated-vapor states and the critical point. Every result is a read-only state (T, P, h, s, density, cp, phase, quality). It runs on CoolProp’s HEOS backend, with the faster BICUBIC tables as an option.

Results against the plan’s checks:

  • Round-trips: all 18 tests pass. The hypothesis round-trips hold to 1e-6 for both fluids. A separate 20,000-point stress test per fluid had zero failures, with a worst error of about 1e-9.
  • Critical points: they match the published equation-of-state values from Setzmann & Wagner (methane) and Lemmon et al. (air). I tested against those papers’ numbers rather than CoolProp’s own output, since comparing CoolProp to itself proves nothing.
  • Speed: 10k lookups take 0.39 s for Methane and 0.31 s for Air, against a 1 s target.

What I found in CoolProp (details in docs/DECISIONS.md D20–D24):

  • Air’s two-phase region needed a workaround (D21). CoolProp refuses Air lookups by temperature inside its condensing range. Near the bubble line, its enthalpy-based lookup also contradicts its own saturation data: it failed or reported “liquid” for states that are actually 0.7% vapor. This region is exactly where E-1 condenses air, so I made the module decide the phase itself using CoolProp’s saturation data, which is exact for this air model. I also corrected the plan’s risk note, since this air model does have a condensing glide.
  • Methane’s valid range tops out at 625 K (D20). CoolProp carries on above that without any error, so later phases will flag results above 625 K. The low end is the melting line rather than the plan’s 90 K, and anything below it now raises an error.
  • Flashes at exactly the critical pressure fail (D22). The module nudges the pressure up by one part in a million and retries.
  • BICUBIC table cache (D23). CoolProp would normally write its tables to ~/.CoolProp. They now go to the repo’s git-ignored var/, to keep rule 7.

Robustness:

  • Grid: the answer is the same to 3 decimals with 50, 200 or 800 segments.

Why it works: the pinch is inside E-1, just above the hot stream’s dew point, where the pumped supercritical fluid’s heat-capacity spike meets the condensing vapor. That’s the mechanism the plan describes. One consequence: the intermediate pressure doesn’t depend on T-1 inlet temperature, T-1 efficiency, or recuperation before E-1. That’s good news for Phase 4, because the Fig. 1 recuperators won’t move it.

What’s built:

PromptSep 26 · 00:51 UTC

ClaudeSep 26 · 00:52 UTCI'll use Fig. 1, the simplest case. Every number below comes from the model at Fig. 1's default inputs. You can follow along on the Flowsheet tab: pass 1 is the top row and pass 2 the bottom row, and clicking a unit…

I’ll use Fig. 1, the simplest case. Every number below comes from the model at Fig. 1’s default inputs. You can follow along on the Flowsheet tab: pass 1 is the top row and pass 2 the bottom row, and clicking a unit shows its numbers.

The setting

An LNG terminal stores natural gas as a liquid at about −259 °F. Before the gas can go into a pipeline, it has to be pumped to pipeline pressure and warmed into a gas. Normally that warming happens in big vaporizers heated by seawater or air, and the LNG’s “cold” is thrown away.

The patent’s idea is to run the gas itself through a power cycle on its way to the pipeline, and to use its own coldness to do it twice. In the model, 10 kg/s (about 79,000 lb/h) of LNG flows through the cycle. A gas turbine’s exhaust at 1000 °F provides the heat.

The key trick first

Pumping a liquid takes very little energy, and compressing a gas takes a lot. A turbine produces power by letting high-pressure gas expand to a lower pressure. To expand the same gas a second time, you first have to bring it back up to high pressure. As a gas, that needs an expensive compressor. If you can turn the gas back into a liquid, a small pump does the job.

Turning gas back into liquid needs something very cold to condense it against. That’s the incoming LNG. So the cold LNG entering the plant condenses the gas that has just come out of the first turbine. That’s why the cycle is called “re-condensing”.

Pass 1: pump, heat, expand

Step Unit What happens After it
1 P-1, LNG pump Pumps liquid from the tank to high pressure. It’s still a liquid, so this costs little: 0.32 MW −250 °F, 1500 psia
2 E-1, shared exchanger Picks up heat from the T-1 exhaust (see below). This is where the incoming liquid does its condensing job −23 °F
3 H-1, warming exchanger Warms it to about ambient (heat from water or air) 75 °F
4 S-1, splitter → E-2 / E-3 Half is heated by the gas turbine exhaust (E-2), half by the hot T-1 exhaust (E-3, a “recuperator”) 442 °F
5 M-1 → E-4 The two halves rejoin. The hottest part of the gas turbine exhaust tops the flow up 600 °F, 1500 psia
6 T-1, turbine Expands the gas to the intermediate pressure and produces power: 2.44 MW 447 °F, 523 psia

Between the passes: cool it down and condense it

The T-1 exhaust is still hot, 447 °F, and that heat is useful, so it’s used before the gas is condensed:

Step Unit What happens After it
7 E-3 Gives heat back to pass 1 (step 4) 257 °F
8 E-5 Gives heat to pass 2 (step 12 below) 80 °F
9 E-1 Condenses completely against the incoming LNG from step 2 −131 °F, liquid
10 V-1, accumulator A small holding vessel for the liquid

Why 523 psia? The lower T-1’s outlet pressure, the more power T-1 makes. But lower pressure also means the gas condenses at a colder temperature, and eventually the incoming LNG isn’t cold enough to condense it. The model finds the lowest pressure at which E-1 can still fully condense the gas, keeping at least a 5 °F temperature difference between the two sides everywhere in the exchanger. That’s 523 psia here. The patent mentions about 520 psia for LNG, so the model agrees within about 0.6 %. This point where the two sides come closest is the “pinch” marked on the E-1 temperature profile chart.

Pass 2: pump again, heat, expand again

Step Unit What happens After it
11 P-2, pump Pumps the liquid from 523 to 2000 psia. 0.48 MW, far less than compressing a gas −97 °F, 2000 psia
12 H-2 → E-5 Warmed to ambient, then heated by the T-1 exhaust (step 8) 221 °F
13 T-2, turbine Second expansion, down to pipeline pressure: 0.96 MW 129 °F, 1000 psia
14 PL Into the pipeline, which is where the gas had to go anyway

The gas turbine exhaust, meanwhile, goes through E-4 then E-2 and leaves the stack at 536 °F (1000 → 777 → 536 °F).

The result

MW
Turbines: T-1 2.44 + T-2 0.96 3.40
Pumps: P-1 0.32 + P-2 0.48 −0.81
Net power 2.60
Baseline: pump → heat → a single expansion, same LNG flow 1.24

The same gas, delivered to the same pipeline, produces about twice the net power, because it’s expanded twice and the second pressurization costs only a pump. The heat comes from exhaust that would otherwise go up the stack, plus ambient warming that the terminal would need anyway.

Two points to keep in mind:

  • Pass 2 is heated only to 221 °F, using leftover heat from the T-1 exhaust, so T-2 makes less power than T-1. More heat on pass 2 would raise its output, which is one of the trade-offs the Sweeps and Optimize tools let you explore.
  • The defaults are generic, feasible sizings, not a particular plant or vendor’s equipment. Treat the numbers as showing how the cycle behaves, not as a performance guarantee.

  • At a peak-shaving plant, pipeline gas is liquefied when demand is low and sent out when it’s high. The stored gas is the main product, and the electricity is a bonus. The patent describes its LNG cases this way, as combined gas and power storage.
