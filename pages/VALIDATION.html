Phase 3 — E-1 intermediate pressure (2026-09-25)

Conditions. The patent doesn’t state these, so the D15 defaults are used and were not tuned:

  • Tank: saturated liquid at 14.696 psia (“at or near atmospheric pressure”, col. 5 ll. 25-26).
  • P-1 discharge 1500 psia (patent range 1000-2500 psia, col. 5 ll. 36-37); pump η_s 0.75.
  • T-1 inlet 300 °F at the P-1 discharge pressure; T-1 η_s 0.85. No pressure drops.
  • E-1 ΔT_min 5 °F, N = 200 equal-duty segments plus phase-change nodes, with the pinch refined.
  • 100 % of the pumped fluid re-condensed (m_hot = m_cold), as the patent assumes (col. 3 ll. 28-31).
  • E-1 hot inlet = T-1 exhaust (Fig. 3 arrangement). This does not affect the result; see below.

Where the pinch is. For both fluids the pinch is internal, a little into the hot stream’s desuperheating zone just above its dew point. There the cold stream (supercritical, near its pseudo-critical cp peak) meets the hot vapor. The cold end is not the pinch: it has 80+ °F of approach. The pinch doesn’t depend on how much superheat the hot stream carries, so P_int is independent of the T-1 inlet temperature (identical from 50 °F to 900 °F) and of T-1 efficiency. It is also unchanged by recuperating the T-1 exhaust before E-1 (Fig. 1’s exchangers 114/122), as long as the E-1 hot inlet stays superheated.

Modeling caveats

  • LNG is pure methane (PLAN.md §6). Real LNG’s heavier components would raise the bubble point and add a condensing glide.
  • Air is CoolProp’s pseudo-pure model (D21): it has a bubble/dew glide, but it isn’t a true mixture.

How to read the gain. The baseline follows PLAN.md §1: pump → warm → heat → single expansion → out, with no recuperator. Its expander exhaust goes to the pipeline hot, so part of Fig. 1’s advantage is the recuperation (114/122) that the double-expansion layout makes possible. This shows up as Fig. 1 needing less heat-source input (5.76 vs 8.98 MW) while making twice the net power. The baseline pump discharge is max(P-1, P-2) = 2000 psia (D38).

Observations, not tuned

  • Fig. 1’s pipeline gas leaves T-2 at 128.8 °F; the baseline’s leaves its expander at 496.3 °F. Real pipelines often cap delivery temperature, and that isn’t modeled.
  • The T-1 tear converges in one iteration because every heater has a temperature spec, so nothing downstream feeds back numerically. Real recycle coupling arrives with the heat-source streams in Phase 5.

How to read the gain. The baseline (pump → warm → heat to the HP-turbine inlet temperature → one expansion to 15.2 psia) burns no fuel and has no ORC, so the Fig. 4 advantage includes the duct burner’s 3.03 MW of fuel and the ORC. The fuel heat rate is reported so the two can be judged together.

Patent sanity check: “about 1.5 times the power of the gas turbine generator 432” (col. 19 ll. 1-4). This can’t be checked without a GT rating. The patent names a Siemens SGT-800 as an example, and we use no vendor data (D46). The ratio is reported in extra.system_over_gt when the user supplies gt_power.

Observations, not tuned

  • Default sizing leaves heat unused: the ORC is condenser-limited, so its exhaust share leaves at 468 °F, and recuperator 414 does almost nothing. A real design would rebalance the exhaust split, ORC pressures and HP turbine inlet. That is a job for the Phase 6 optimizer, not for tuning the defaults here.
  • The system exit (80 °F after recuperator 410) is below the patent’s “about 100 °F to 250 °F”. Max-duty recuperation at a 5 °F approach cools it further than the patent’s description.

  • Fig. 1’s LNG cycle has an energy ratio of about 5.5. It returns about 0.072 kWh per kg against the 0.4 kWh/kg paid to liquefy it, so power-only arbitrage barely pays even with perfect foresight, and the fixed rules lose money. That follows from the model’s physics and the assumptions below; it is not a solver bug. The patent’s LNG cases pair the power with gas storage (Fig. 5 is “a gas and electric energy storage system storing natural gas as LNG at times of excess gas and electric energy and returning both electric power to the grid and gas to the pipeline system”, col. 20 ll. 37-41), and the stored gas’s own value is outside this power-only dispatch model (D65).
  • Liquid air (Fig. 4) has an energy ratio of about 1.9 with these placeholder inputs and is profitable under all three rules.

How to read this comparison (added 2026-09-26). It does not show that liquid air is a better business than LNG. It shows only that, when the liquid must be made with purchased electricity and only electricity is sold, the fuel-assisted liquid-air case recovers more of that electricity than the unfired LNG case.

  • The charging assumption fits liquid air, not LNG. Liquid air has no value of its own, so buying electricity to liquefy it is the real business. At an LNG import terminal the LNG arrives already liquefied and must be regasified anyway, so the power is extra income from a step that happens regardless. At a peak-shaving plant the stored gas is the main product. In both cases most of the value is the gas, which this model doesn’t count (D65).
  • The two cases are not like for like. Fig. 4 burns about 3.0 MW of fuel (LHV) in B-1 and adds an ORC (0.73 MW net); Fig. 1 burns nothing. The energy ratio ignores fuel, which flatters Fig. 4. Fuel is charged in net revenue, but at the placeholder $4/GJ.
  • Part of Fig. 1’s gap is design, not fluid. Its pass 2 is heated only by T-1 exhaust, so T-2’s inlet is 221 °F and T-2 makes 0.96 MW against T-1’s 2.44 MW. Neither default design is optimized.
  • The inputs are placeholders: the same 0.4 kWh/kg liquefaction energy for both fluids, synthetic prices, and no vendor data.
  • A fair LNG evaluation needs an LNG dispatch mode: no electricity charge for liquefaction (or the cost of gas bought to liquefy), revenue for gas sent to the pipeline, and the electricity as added value. Real inputs (gas prices, send-out rates, liquefaction energy) should come from actual projects. A fluid-only comparison would also need both fluids run on the same heat source without fuel.
